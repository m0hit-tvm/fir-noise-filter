How it works (for the technically curious)
  1. The sensor is sampled 1000 times per second. A Fourier transform turns the recording into its frequency "recipe", which shows a 50 Hz tone, a 120 Hz tone and a flat noise floor.
  2. A low-pass FIR filter is designed with the windowed-sinc method: the ideal filter's impulse response is truncated to 101 taps and shaped with a Hamming window. The taps match scipy.signal.firwin to machine precision.
  3. The filter is applied by direct convolution. Because the taps are symmetric, every frequency is delayed by exactly 50 samples, so the output is shifted back to line up with the input.
  4. Quality is scored as a signal-to-noise ratio against the known clean signal. The jump from about 4 dB to about 16 dB is a 16x reduction in error power.

Project 2 · Mechanical vibration

Hearing how a machine part vibrates from a single tap

Tap a wine glass and it rings at its own note, then fades. Machine parts do the same. From one hammer tap and a noisy accelerometer, the program works out the part's natural frequency and how quickly it stops ringing, two numbers engineers need to avoid destructive resonance.

How it works (for the technically curious)
  1. The part is modelled as a mass-spring-damper: m = 2 kg, k = 8000 N/m, c = 6 N·s/m. Theory gives a natural frequency of 10.07 Hz and a damping ratio of 0.0237.
  2. The hammer hit is a 2 ms half-sine pulse. The equation of motion is integrated numerically and 10 % sensor noise is added to make a realistic "measurement".
  3. The natural frequency is read from the peak of a zero-padded FFT of the ring-down. No window is used, because a decaying signal is already its own window.
  4. Damping is estimated two ways: the logarithmic decrement (a least-squares fit to how the peaks shrink) and the half-power bandwidth of the FFT peak. Both land within about 1 % of the true value.
  5. The frequency response function X/F = 1 / (k − mω² + jcω) shows the resonance and its amplification factor Q = 1/(2ζ).
