One tap tells you how a part rings.

Tap a wine glass and it sings at its own note, then fades. Machine parts do the same. From a single hammer hit and a noisy sensor, this project works out the part's natural frequency and how fast it stops ringing: the two numbers engineers need to keep it clear of destructive resonance.

How it works

For readers who want the engineering.

Method, in five steps
  1. The part is modelled as a mass-spring-damper: m = 2 kg, k = 8000 N/m, c = 6 N·s/m. Theory gives a natural frequency of 10.07 Hz and a damping ratio of 0.0237.
  2. The hammer hit is a 2 ms half-sine pulse. The equation of motion is integrated numerically and 10 % sensor noise is added to make a realistic measurement.
  3. The natural frequency is read from the peak of a zero-padded FFT of the ring-down. No window is used, because a decaying signal is already its own window.
  4. Damping is estimated two ways: the logarithmic decrement (a least-squares fit to how the peaks shrink) and the half-power bandwidth of the FFT peak. Both land within about 1 % of the true value.
  5. The frequency response function X/F = 1 / (k − mω² + jcω) shows the resonance and its amplification factor Q = 1/(2ζ).
